Keeping pace without AI? The AI-free caucus on arXiv

The Association for Human Mathematics lists 809 mathematicians who commit to not publishing texts generated by AI, and 532 of them state that they do not use AI models in their research at all. I compare the arXiv output of these mathematicians with that of all other authors of arXiv mathematics papers, following an analysis plan that was fixed before any result was computed. From the five years before ChatGPT to the two years after the first reasoning models, the output per author of the AI-free group grew by 15%, almost exactly as much as that of comparable mathematicians, whose output grew by 17%. Only in the summer of 2026, when the number of all arXiv mathematics papers suddenly doubled, did the comparable mathematicians pull ahead, with an interval that only just excludes no difference. The AI-free group mentions AI tools less often, writes more coauthored papers than before and is closely connected by coauthorship.

A short proof of Bugeaud's bound for Sturmian digit strings

I give a short proof of Bugeaud's S-part bound for integers with Sturmian digit strings. The argument uses the p-adic Subspace Theorem in dimensions two and three and also applies to finite words of linear factor complexity.

Digit sums11 October 2026Version 27 pages

When a number is the digit sum of its own power

I study positive integers satisfying n = s(nk), where s is the decimal digit sum. A congruence condition and a random digit model explain patterns in the solutions. The model suggests infinitely many pairs and triples of consecutive exponents. I conjecture that 90 is the only n > 1 with four consecutive exponents. In other bases, an explicit construction gives arbitrarily long runs.

Mixed products of strongly pseudoperfect numbers

A number n is strongly pseudoperfect if some set of its divisors is closed under d ↦ n/d and adds up to 2n. I show that two such numbers a and b whose divisor sets contain 1 and n can be mixed into a2b, even when they are not coprime. This gives infinitely many primitive odd examples with only three prime factors, including ones of Euler's form, and so answers Questions 18 and 20 of Wang and Zelinsky. I also count these examples and give two shorter proofs of their results.
